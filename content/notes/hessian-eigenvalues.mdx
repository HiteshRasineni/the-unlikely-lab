Training a machine-learning model means descending a loss surface. The tools that describe that surface are classical multivariable calculus and linear algebra.

## The Gradient

For a scalar loss function $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ over parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the gradient collects all partial derivatives:

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_d} \right)
$$

It points in the direction of steepest *increase*; gradients therefore flow *downhill* by stepping against it:

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)
$$

with learning rate $\eta$. Backpropagation is simply an efficient algorithm for computing $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ through deep compositions.

## The Hessian

Whereas the gradient gives slope, the Hessian gives curvature — the matrix of second derivatives:

$$
H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_i \,\partial \theta_j}
$$

Locally, a second-order Taylor expansion describes the landscape as

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta + \delta) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta) + \nabla \mathcal{L}^\top \delta + \tfrac{1}{2}\, \delta^\top H\, \delta
$$

## Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues

The spectral decomposition of a symmetric matrix is

$$
H v_i = \lambda_i v_i
$$

Eigenvectors $v_i$ identify independent directions of curvature; eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ measure how strongly the surface bends along each one. Their signs carry direct geometric meaning:

- All $\lambda_i > 0$: locally convex — a bowl-like minimum.
- All $\lambda_i < 0$: locally concave — a maximum.
- Mixed signs: a saddle point.
- Some $\lambda_i \approx 0$: nearly flat directions, where the loss barely responds to motion.

In high dimensions these spectra are informative about training dynamics. Loss surfaces of large networks tend to be dominated numerically by flat directions punctuated by a few strong ones — a fact tied to why large models train surprisingly well despite enormous non-convexity.

## Curvature and Optimization

Curvature connects directly to optimizers' behavior:

- **Step-size limits.** Along direction $v$ with eigenvalue $\lambda > 0$, gradient descent diverges if $\eta > 2/\lambda$. Curvature sets the maximum safe learning rate — one reason extreme curvature (vanishing or exploding gradients) destabilizes training.
- **Ill-conditioning.** A wide spread between largest and smallest positive eigenvalues forces compromises: steps small enough for stiff directions become painfully slow along flat ones. This condition-number problem motivates adaptive per-parameter methods such as Adam.
- **Flat minima and generalization.** Empirically, solutions reached in flatter basins often generalize better than those in sharp ravines; sharpness along more directions tends to correlate with sensitivity to perturbations.

## Representation Learning Connection

Curvature ideas reach beyond optimization:

- The same eigendecomposition appears in second-order methods, Gauss–Newton approximations, and trust-region steps used to stabilize flow-model training.
- Principal component analysis finds eigenvectors of data covariance matrices: principal directions of variance, interpretable as the most descriptive axes of a dataset's feature space.
- Spectral properties of learned representations — conditioning, effective rank, spread of singular values — shape how information flows through deep architectures, including autoregressive networks inside flows.
