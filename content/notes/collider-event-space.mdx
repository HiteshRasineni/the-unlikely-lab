## Events as Vectors

Strip away the detectors, the reconstruction chains, and the physics language, and a collision event — after analysis — is a vector of numbers:

$$
\mathbf{x} = (p_T^{(1)}, \eta^{(1)}, \phi^{(1)}, \ldots,\; p_T^{(n)}, \eta^{(n)}, \phi^{(n)},\; E_T^{\text{miss}}, \; \ldots)
$$

transverse momenta, angles, invariant masses of reconstructed objects, missing transverse momentum. Ten, twenty, fifty components depending on the analysis. See [From Four-Vectors to Physical Observables](/notes/four-vectors) for where these entries come from.

## Feature Space

This vector defines a point in a **feature space**: one axis per observable, one point per recorded event. A dataset of millions of collisions is a cloud of points in this space, with structure inherited directly from the underlying physical processes:

- Leptonic Z decays cluster near the Z boson mass shell.
- QCD multijet production populates broad, strongly correlated regions.
- Specific decay chains trace out characteristic manifolds.

Feature engineering is nothing more than choosing coordinates for this space such that known physics appears geometrically. An invariant mass, for instance, becomes a radial-like coordinate whose bulk value reveals particle resonances.

## Correlations

The coordinates of real events are far from independent. Energy conservation correlates total momenta; decay kinematics correlate angles and masses; detector acceptance carves correlated boundaries into the accessible region. Any honest model of event distributions must capture this correlation structure — which is why methods limited to modeling each variable marginally, in isolation, discard most of the physics.

## High-Dimensional Distributions

Taken together, the cloud of points defines an empirical distribution in high-dimensional space. Density estimation [attempts to learn](/notes/density-estimation) that distribution: a function assigning plausible "bulk density" values to ordinary configurations, shaped by all correlations at once.

This is where modern flow-based models earn their complexity. Their layered invertible transformations can warp a simple base distribution into shapes matching genuinely complicated, multimodal, correlated target densities.

## Connection to Anomaly Detection

Once a density $p(\mathbf{x})$ is learned over event space, anomaly detection becomes geometry:

- Ordinary events live in high-density regions of the learned distribution.
- Rare or unexpected processes — or artifacts — appear as outliers with low density.

Ranking events by [likelihood-derived scores](/notes/anomaly-tails) therefore maps a statistical question ("what should ordinary look like?") onto a search strategy ("what doesn't look like it?"). The quality of the answer inherits directly from the fidelity of the learned density — hence the emphasis on careful density estimation before any talk of anomalies.
