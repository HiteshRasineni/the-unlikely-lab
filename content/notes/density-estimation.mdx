Density estimation is the problem of learning a probability distribution from data. Given a set of observations, we want a function that tells us how probable each new observation is — not just on average, but point by point across the entire space of possibilities.

## Probability Distributions and Density Functions

A probability distribution assigns probabilities to outcomes. When outcomes are continuous — such as the invariant mass of a reconstructed particle — we describe the distribution with a **probability density function** $p(x)$, where

$$
\int_a^b p(x)\,dx = P(a \le X \le b)
$$

and $p$ integrates to one over its whole support. The value $p(x)$ at a single point is not itself a probability; only integrals over regions are. This distinction matters constantly in physics analysis.

For multivariate data — an event described by many observables simultaneously — the density becomes a function of many variables, $p(\mathbf{x}) = p(x_1, \dots, x_d)$, and correlations between variables are part of what must be learned.

## Empirical Distributions

Given $N$ samples, the simplest model of their distribution is the empirical distribution: assign each observation equal weight. This is exact but useless for generalization — between two observed points it says nothing, and outside the sampled region it says nothing at all. Any useful density estimate must interpolate and extrapolate sensibly.

## Parametric versus Non-parametric

**Parametric** methods assume a functional form with fixed complexity — a Gaussian, a Poisson, an exponential family — and estimate parameters:

$$
p(x \mid \theta) \quad \text{with } \theta \text{ estimated from data}
$$

These are efficient when the form is correct, and badly wrong when it is not. Real collider event distributions rarely obey any small family of forms.

**Non-parametric** methods let the data determine the shape of the density: histograms, kernel density estimation, nearest-neighbor methods. They are more flexible but scale poorly as dimension grows.

Between these two extremes sit modern machine-learning models — including normalizing flows — which use flexible parametric families (neural networks) that can approximate very general distributions.

## Why High Dimensions Are Hard

The difficulty of density estimation grows exponentially with dimension, a phenomenon related to the *curse of dimensionality*:

- The volume of the space concentrates far from the origin; almost all of the space is empty.
- Fixed numbers of samples become vanishingly sparse.
- The number of histogram bins needed to cover the space explodes.

A histogram of one observable might need tens of bins. A joint distribution over twenty observables needs astronomically many cells. This is exactly why learned, structured models — flows with inductive biases built into their architecture — matter for high-energy physics data.

## Connection to Collider Event Data

In collider physics, each collision event gives us a vector of measured quantities: momenta of leptons and jets, missing transverse momentum, invariant masses. A good density estimate over this event space means knowing *what ordinary events look like* — and once we know that, anything that lives where the density is low becomes interesting.

This is the core logic connecting density estimation to anomaly detection: learn the expected, then look for the unexpected.
