It looks like the same formula. It behaves like something entirely different.

## Probability

Probability describes how plausible *data* is, given a fixed model. For a discrete random variable,

$$
P(X = x) \ge 0, \qquad \sum_x P(X=x) = 1
$$

Probabilities over the possible outcomes sum — or integrate, for continuous variables — to one. Probability is a function of the *outcome*, holding the model fixed.

## Probability Density

For a continuous variable there is no probability at a point, only a density $p(x)$: a function whose integral over any region gives the probability that the outcome lands in that region. Densities can exceed one; only their integrals are probabilities. This alone breaks many naive intuitions about what "$p(x)$" means numerically.

## Likelihood

The likelihood turns this around. It asks: given that we observed particular data $\mathbf{x}$, how well do different model parameters $\theta$ explain it?

$$
L(\theta) = p(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta)
$$

The right-hand side has exactly the same functional form as probability or density — that is why the confusion persists. But as a function it now treats $\mathbf{x}$ as *fixed* and varies $\theta$.

Crucially, likelihoods are not normalized over $\theta$. Nothing forces $\int L(\theta)\,d\theta = 1$; often the integral does not even exist. Comparing likelihood values is meaningful, reading them as absolute probabilities is not.

## Why the Distinction Matters

This is not pedantry. The distinction changes what you can legitimately conclude:

1. **Scales of likelihood differ.** In high-dimensional continuous problems, log-likelihood values can be large negative numbers determined mostly by dimensional volume factors — irrelevant constants when comparing models but misleading if read as "how likely the event is."

2. **Relative statements only.** A likelihood supports statements like "parameter value $\theta_1$ explains the data better than $\theta_2$" via ratios. It does not support "the probability of $\theta$ is 0.03" without additional Bayesian machinery and priors.

3. **Model comparison requires care.** Likelihoods from models with different functional forms or different numbers of parameters cannot be compared naively.

4. **Likelihood-based anomaly detection.** When an event receives a low density score under a learned background model, the number is a density — tied to the specific feature space and normalization chosen. What it licenses is a ranking of events within that space, not an absolute statement about how "rare" reality has become. Cross-checks against independent quantities remain necessary.
